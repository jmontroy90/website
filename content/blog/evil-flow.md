(Sidebar: Long time! I'd like to blog more. So here we go.)

I've been interested in the idea of "evil flow" recently - a kind of bizarro flow state where you DO get things done and time DOES fly, but 
it doesn't feel particularly...joyful. I'd describe the feeling as stubborn, hamster-wheel-like tunnel vision, where the world collapses into a singular focus and the goal-seeker is 
more hound-like than human. My body tenses, my cadence gets clipped, my tone harried. I get the job done, and feel no relief, no joy, just grim satisfaction at most.

(Note: I get into this state most frequently during tech debugging deep dives, and I suspect that capitalism 
has **loved** this trait of mine. Someone once told me I became a "whole different person" when I talked about my job circa 2017. It didn't feel too bad to hear at the time, but I look 
back now and wince.)

Meanwhile, good ol' regular flow states are markedly different. I make progress with a sense of excitement. I zoom out as much as I zoom in. I take notes and flag things for the future. I am 
curious about everything. I feel a sense of abundance and overflow. I notice details and find joy in them. I feel 
immense gratitude for the work of others. I feel love for humanity's insanely optimistic tendency to structure information for the consumption of others, with such nuanced, loving 
thought given to the balance between depth and approachability. I love discovering the quirks and details of other people's structuring, where I can see their heart almost readily as their brain. 
And when I explain my work to others, I do it with waterfall-like giddiness, whereas evil flow explains itself with resounding animus and thinly-veiled grievance.

### Gaming vs. Playing

Hard turn - on a recent (brutal, 34+-hour-travel-day) flight, I finished two pieces of content -- DOOM: The Dark Ages and Toy Story 5.

I love the modern DOOM games. They're so lovingly refined 
beneath their
absurd, hyper-masculine, adrenaline-pumping veneers. Playing DOOM: Eternal often felt closer to dancing. I suspect that sentiment might strike some people as absurd, but frankly, IYKYK.

The DOOM games are peak gaming. They set you out on a goal - clear this room of hellspawn - and then say to you, "go, fly! become competent! refine your skills! technical proficiency is the 
meaning of life!". I love games like this. Celeste is, I think, similar in this. Hades too, perhaps. They provide with a wonderful world of tools and competencies to master, and then fling you 
through 
gauntlets. So fun. So addictive. Very satisfying.

But. Maybe it's kinda evil flow?

Enter Toy Story 5 (mild spoilers ahead). A lovely movie, dedicated to a balanced, thoughtful take on modern tech replacing toys. There's a line in it - I can't recall it perfectly - but maybe it's 
Jessie who sees kids hooked on their gadgets all day, and says "that there isn't playing, that's just gaming!". Ultimately, the gadgets, with their full Pixar personalities, come to agree, and 
deploy themselves as tools in service of the real goal of childhood - play.

I think DOOM (and Hades, and partially Celeste) might be pure gaming, and I suspect pure gaming is kinda close to evil flow. Oh god are they good games. But I finished DOOM and felt very little beyond 
fleeting 
satisfaction. I've played SO much Celeste, and one of the reasons I think the game is special is because it's peak platformer gameplay - the experience of playing it is riveting and 
satisfying.

But that's not what makes it one of my favorite games. I remember the beautiful music, the delicate atmosphere, the minimal but moving story of self-overcoming, the stopped mountain lift and the 
feather exercise. I remember the things that took me out of tunnel vision mode and helped me zoom out, and feel wonder, joy, notice details.

Compare this to games that I think are actual play. Outer Wilds, The Last of Us Part 2, Red Dead Redemption 2, Breath of the Wild.  When I play these games, I become a more inquisitive human _in real 
life_. I am encouraged to explore, experiment, and play, and in the process I notice details, pick up 
on structure, and so on. After long sessions with these games, I become more observant as I move through the world - I notice the design of informational graphics on subways, take the extra moment 
to ask that question about some food I've been eating, or how car engines work, or what the differences in stop light wait times are around the world and why.

These games all come with some friction, which I think is pretty important. RDR2 is wildly slow and careful. BotW requires experimentation. Outer Wilds, I mean god, enough said - the whole game is 
dedicated to 
discovery and the friction that can come with (blog post coming on that soon, I think). The Last of Us Part II literally FORCES you to empathy. Friction, across the board. And yet these games will 
be with me forever. They are art, they are play.

(The ultimate measure of this theory, for me, is how little I recall of the DOOM games once I finish them. Mind you, these games are BEAUTIFUL - so full of stunning, epic set pieces that it's almost 
too much. And I look at almost none of it. Sometimes I step back and take it in, but mostly I don't. Sometimes I almost get ambushed by the details of the game - wait, is that a full, giant devil's 
skull above the door? That looks awesome. I bet you someone took extra time to specifically make that, and justified to their team why it would be sick and appropriate for this level.)

### So what?

I love gaming - the low-friction, highly-addictive tunnel-vision evil flow that game-y games can induce is stimulating, fun, and a totally lovely use of some of my time. And all these games have 
such care and love put into them.

But those kinds of games are built for gaming, not playing - competence-based, task-focused, relentlessly driving you forward. And I think maybe a balanced video game diet needs a little less gaming 
and a 
little more play / discovery / curiosity. A little less evil flow, that flattens the world and obviates detail, and a little more joyful flow. A 
little more friction, a little more slowness. So maybe as I proceed forward in my gaming selection, I choose the vegetables of puzzles games, or classic RPGs, and sprinkle in the rapid-fire game-y 
games a little less often. (Which is actually exactly what I did - I finished RDR2, started Disco Elysium, and said "phew, I need something dumb and fast", and so played DOOM.) 

But I think what I'm 
newly reckoning with, if anything, is that some of the games I've logged the most time into might be things that ultimately don't leave me full, don't expand me, don't ground me in the world (both 
virtual 
and real-life). And I definitely get the most out of the games that expand me, despite the friction they often come with.

So in conclusion: choose play, and welcome the friction it often comes with. Be wary of ease and evil flow. Something something AI is the death of us. Yes.
